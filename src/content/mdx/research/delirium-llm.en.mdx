Delirium is common in intensive care, but the clinical variables used to predict
it differ substantially across studies. This study evaluated whether large
language models can prioritize variables for incident ICU delirium **without
access to patient outcomes**, treating literature-level model knowledge as a
potential prior for feature selection rather than as a substitute for empirical
labels.

Eleven delirium-prediction studies were reviewed to define 107 clinical features,
which were operationalized as 151 analysis-ready MIMIC-IV variables. The final
leakage-aware cohort contained **32,574 first ICU stays**, including 2,728 cases
of delirium first documented after the initial 24-hour observation window. This
design separated baseline predictor collection from the incident outcome period
and reduced the risk of including variables recorded after delirium onset.

Four feature-selection procedures were evaluated: Scoring, Ranking, Sequencing,
and log-prior. GPT-4.1, Claude Sonnet 4.5, and Gemini 2.5 Pro were evaluated with Scoring,
Ranking, and Sequencing prompts. Each model–procedure combination was repeated
five times to quantify run-to-run stability; a log-prior procedure was also tested
with GPT-4.1. Reproducibility was assessed using rank agreement and top-k overlap,
and selected subsets were benchmarked with stratified five-fold XGBoost models.

The prompting procedure influenced reproducibility more strongly than the choice
of LLM, with Scoring producing the most stable rankings. The best 30-feature LLM
subset retained **93.0% of the full-feature XGBoost AUROC**, supporting the
potential of LLMs as assistive tools for feature prioritisation in clinical
prediction research, especially before a fully labeled cohort is available.
